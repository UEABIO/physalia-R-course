The chart-type table

Before anything else, the structure of your data determines the chart type. Variable count and variable type narrow the choice. The table below is a guide rather than a rule: your scientific question may demand something else, but you should be able to justify it.

Variable 1 Variable 2 Variable 3 Default chart
Continuous — — Raincloud (ggdist::stat_halfeye + boxplot + jittered points)
Continuous Continuous — Scatter plot
Continuous Continuous Discrete Scatter plot with colour and shape mapped to the discrete variable
Discrete — — Bar chart of counts
Discrete Continuous — Raincloud per group
Discrete Discrete — Heatmap of counts
Discrete Discrete Continuous Heatmap of the continuous variable
Date Continuous — Line chart
Date Continuous Discrete Line chart, one line per group

A few rules are worth memorising: no pie charts (use a labelled proportion); no 3D effects of any kind; no dual y-axes (use two panels instead); no dynamite plots (bar charts of means with error bars) where raw data are available, so show a raincloud or strip plot instead (Weissgerber et al., 2015).

Chart chooser. Use the table to pick a default.

Wing length of 40 butterflies from each of three sites:

Raincloud per group. A continuous measurement in three groups: the raincloud shows every point and the shape of each distribution. A bar of means with error bars hides both.

Daily soil temperature over a year at two field stations:

Line chart, one line per group. A continuous variable over time: one line per station shows the seasonal pattern and lets readers compare the stations directly.

Number of nests found in each habitat × year combination:

Heatmap of counts. Counts in a grid of two categorical variables: a heatmap shows the whole table at once. Dual axes and 3D bars distort the comparison.

Descriptive vs explanatory framing

Every figure communicates at two levels: it presents the data, and it makes an argument. The framing of the title and the legend determines which level the reader engages with first.

A descriptive title states what the figure shows in neutral language: “Body mass of three penguin species in the Palmer Archipelago”. The reader is shown the data and invited to interpret.

An explanatory title states what the figure means: “Gentoo penguins are substantially heavier than Adelie and Chinstrap”. The reader is told the conclusion and invited to verify it from the data.

Neither is wrong. The choice is determined by the audience. Journal figures and lab reports use descriptive framing because the reader is expected to bring their own judgement and the figure legend below carries the necessary methodological detail. Talks, posters, and public engagement use explanatory framing because the reader has limited time, attention, or technical background to derive the conclusion themselves.

Audience design

The same finding becomes a different figure for different audiences. The table below summarises the design choices that should change with context:

Element Journal / lab report Poster Talk Public engagement
Title above figure No Yes (explanatory) Yes (explanatory) Yes (explanatory)
Figure legend below Yes (four elements) Optional, abbreviated No No
Direct labelling Where it does not break convention Yes Yes Yes
Highlighting (grey out non-focal groups) No Yes Yes Yes
Reference lines and annotations Sparingly Yes Yes Yes
Statistical detail Precise p-values where shown Abbreviated One key result None or one sentence
Base font size 12pt (floor) 16–20pt 14–18pt 14–18pt
Raw data shown Always where n permits Always Always Where it clarifies
Interactivity (hover, zoom) Supplementary only No No Yes, on the web

Every figure must stay honest and accessible whatever the audience. What changes are the design choices needed to communicate with that audience.

Interactive figures

On a web page, a lab website, or a Quarto HTML report, the reader can interact with a figure. ggiraph turns a ggplot into an interactive one with almost no new code: swap geom_point() for geom_point_interactive(), add a tooltip (what appears on hover) and a data_id (what gets highlighted together), and wrap the plot in girafe(). Hover over a penguin below:

library(ggiraph)

p <- penguins_complete |>
  ggplot(aes(x = flipper_length_mm, y = body_mass_g, colour = species,
             tooltip = paste0(species, " (", sex, ")\n",
                              body_mass_g, " g, ", island),
             data_id = species)) +
  geom_point_interactive(size = 2, alpha = 0.8) +
  colorspace::scale_colour_discrete_qualitative(palette = "Dark2") +
  labs(x = "Flipper length (mm)", y = "Body mass (g)", colour = "Species") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

girafe(ggobj = p,
       options = list(opts_hover_inv(css = "opacity:0.15;")))

Because data_id = species, hovering on one penguin highlights its whole species and fades the rest: the highlighting row of the audience table, under the reader’s control. Interactivity is a bonus, never a requirement. A figure must still work as a static image, because PDFs, printouts and most journal pages cannot hover.

Exercise: design for an audience

Same data, four audiences

You have one finding:

Gentoo penguins are substantially heavier than Adelie and Chinstrap penguins

Design for an audience (12 minutes). Draw your audience by running sample(1:4, 1), and keep it to yourself for now:

  1. Journal figure (specialist reader, no time pressure)
  2. Conference poster (3 seconds to attract, 2 minutes to engage)
  3. Seminar slide (audience cannot control viewing time)
  4. Museum infographic (non-specialist, no assumed technical knowledge)

Build the best figure of the finding for your audience from penguins_complete, using the audience table above, and leave the audience out of the title. Post a screenshot in Slack. We’ll show a few figures on screen, and everyone posts which audience (1–4) they think it was designed for. That is your test: if people can’t place your figure, it wasn’t clearly designed for anyone. Note which row of the audience table made the biggest difference to your design. (Small group? Build two figures for two different audiences.)

Bonus: if you got the museum, make it interactive with ggiraph. What would a visitor want to see when they hover?
